# Saying "I", using data, and getting the length right

## Before this lesson

Read [STAR in depth](#/lesson/behavioural/star-in-depth) and [STAR variants](#/lesson/behavioural/star-variants). You should know the four STAR parts, why the Action is the largest, and what CARD's "Details" means (concrete quantities such as how many, how much, how long and how often).

## In plain words

Two people describe the same football match. One says: "We played really well and won comfortably." The other says: "I played left back. In the second half their winger kept getting past me, so I asked our midfielder to drop deeper on my side. He did, the winger stopped getting crosses in, and we won 3 to 1." The first sentence tells you the team won. The second tells you what kind of player this person is.

This lesson is about three habits that turn a correct STAR answer into a high-scoring one:

1. **Saying "I"** for your own actions, so the interviewer can see your part.
2. **Using data**: numbers and concrete facts, so the interviewer can see the size of the result.
3. **Getting the length right**, so there is time for several stories and for follow-up questions.

Each sounds small. Together they are the difference between "seems fine, not enough evidence" and "clear hire" in many interviewers' notes, which matters for a job because these notes are what the hiring decision is built on.

## Habit 1: say "I"

### Why "we" hurts you

Amazon's article on common interview mistakes lists consistently saying "we" instead of "I" as one of them. The reason is the scoring process from lesson 3: the interviewer must rate **you**, and "we" gives them nothing to rate. OPM's guide to structured interviews tells interviewers to probe whenever a candidate says "we", which costs interview time you would rather spend on another story.

MIT's career office gives the same advice from the candidate's side: use "I" statements to show what you did, and when the work was a team effort, explain your own contribution to the team's outcome.

### Why people say "we" anyway

- **Modesty.** Many people are raised to see "I did this" as boasting. Saying "we" feels polite and loyal.
- **Real teamwork.** Software is built by teams, and it can feel dishonest to separate one person's part.
- **Habit from work.** In team meetings, "we" is normal and right. Interviews are different: they are about you.

All three are understandable. None changes how you are scored.

### The fix: team, then me

You do not need to stop mentioning the team. You need to make your part visible inside it. A simple pattern works almost every time:

> "The **team's** goal was X. **My** part was Y. **I** did Z."

Examples:

- "Our team of five was migrating the app to a new payment provider. My part was the refund flow. I mapped every refund case in the old system first, and found three that the new provider handled differently..."
- "The club ran a coding workshop for 60 secondary school students. I was in charge of the exercises. I wrote six short tasks, tested each one with two first-year students the week before, and rewrote the two they got stuck on..."

### Giving credit without hiding yourself

Amazon's article also says it is fine to say "I" while also describing what the team achieved. Fair credit is a good sign, as long as it is specific:

- **Good**: "Priya on the data team built the export. I built the screen that used it and the tests for both."
- **Too vague**: "Everyone pitched in and we got it done."

Naming who did what is not boasting. It is accuracy, and accuracy is what the interviewer needs.

### Do not over-correct

The opposite mistake is claiming the team's work as yours. "I built the platform" when you built one screen of it will be exposed by the first follow-up question ("Which part did you find hardest?", "Who decided the database design?"). It also damages trust in everything else you said. Say "I" exactly as much as is true.

### A quick test

Read your story and underline every action verb. For each one, ask: who did this? If the honest answer is "I", the sentence should say "I". If it was someone else, name them or their role. If it was genuinely joint ("we agreed in the meeting"), "we" is correct, but there should be few of those.

## Habit 2: use data

### What "data" means here

**Data**, in an interview answer, means concrete facts that show size and outcome: numbers, dates, durations, counts, before-and-after comparisons, and specific feedback from real people. Amazon's article advises replacing vague answers with specific, quantifiable details, says most examples should be "data-forward" (led by numbers), and adds that when a result is qualitative (about quality rather than quantity), you should bring specific anecdotal feedback instead.

### Where numbers come from

Beginners often say "I do not have any numbers". Usually the numbers exist; nobody has counted them yet. Here are typical sources:

| Setting | Things you can count or measure |
|---|---|
| Software work | Response time, error rate, crash rate, build or test duration, deployment frequency, cloud cost, tickets closed, users affected |
| School projects | Team size, deadline, grade or rank, number of features, test coverage, users in a trial, hours saved for a client |
| Part-time jobs | Queue length, customers per hour, complaints, sales, stock errors, shifts covered |
| Volunteering and clubs | Attendees, sign-ups, money raised, events run, members retained |
| Learning | Time to learn a tool, number of projects built, problems solved |

### The before-and-after pattern

The most persuasive data has a **baseline**: the value before you acted. "The page loads in 0.9 seconds" means little. "The page went from 4 seconds to 0.9 seconds" shows the change you caused. Whenever you mention a result, ask yourself: what was it before?

### Honest estimates

You do not need exact figures. You need honest ones. "About", "roughly" and "around" are fine words: "roughly two thirds of the requests were password resets". If you are not sure, say how you know: "I did not measure it formally, but the queue went from out of the door to about five people on the next busy Saturday."

Never invent a number. Interviewers ask "how did you measure that?", and a made-up figure collapses under that question, taking your credibility with it.

### When the result is qualitative

Some results do not have numbers: a relationship repaired, a confusing document made clear, a teammate's confidence improved. Use specific feedback instead, as Amazon suggests:

- "The client's operations lead wrote to my supervisor that it was the first project handover where they had not needed to call us afterwards."
- "Two months later, the junior developer I had been pairing with asked to lead the next feature on her own."

Specific feedback is data about people.

### Confidential numbers

Some numbers belong to your employer: revenue, user counts, unreleased plans. Do not share them. Use relative numbers instead: "about a 30% drop in failed payments" or "a cost reduction of roughly a fifth". Apple's interview tips warn candidates not to share a previous employer's confidential information; lesson 19 covers this red flag in more detail.

## Habit 3: get the length right

### What the sources say

| Source | Guidance on length |
|---|---|
| Strayer University career centre | One to two minutes per answer |
| Amazon, interview mistakes article | Responses of four minutes or less; interviews typically 45 minutes to an hour |
| Amazon, SDE II preparation page | Four interviews of 55 minutes, each with two to three behavioural questions alongside technical ones |

A practical reading: aim for **about two minutes** for a typical answer, allow up to about three for a complex story (especially at senior levels), and treat four minutes as an absolute ceiling. Interview formats change by company, role and year, so treat these as guides, not rules.

### Why length matters so much

Do the arithmetic for a 45-minute interview. Take away five minutes of introductions and five minutes at the end for your questions. That leaves about 35 minutes. If each of your answers takes six minutes and each attracts three minutes of follow-up questions, the interviewer can cover about four questions. If each answer takes two minutes plus three of follow-ups, they can cover seven. More questions means more competencies covered and more chances to score well. At Amazon, where behavioural questions share an interview with technical work, the pressure on time is even greater.

Long answers also lose the listener. After about two minutes, most people start to forget the beginning of a story.

### Too short is a problem too

A 30-second answer usually skips the Action's reasoning or the Result. It forces the interviewer to drag the evidence out one probe at a time. If your answers are consistently under a minute, you are probably leaving out the "why" behind your steps.

### Offering depth instead of including it

The best way to stay short without losing depth is to **offer** detail rather than include it: "I chose a message queue for this; I am happy to go into why if that is useful." The interviewer can take you up on it if it matters to their rubric, and you have shown that you have more to give.

### Reading the interviewer

Watch for signals during your answer:

- **They are typing steadily**: you are giving usable evidence. Keep going at the same pace.
- **They stop typing and look at you**: you may have drifted into background. Move to your next action or to the result.
- **They interrupt with a question**: answer it directly; do not return to where you were unless they ask.
- **They glance at the clock**: wrap up with the result in one or two sentences.

## Putting the three habits together

Here is a **fictional** answer before and after applying all three habits. The question: "Tell me about a time you improved something at work."

**Before (about 3 minutes when spoken, trimmed here):**
"So at my last company we had this reporting system that was really slow and everyone complained about it, and it had been like that for ages, since before I joined actually, and we decided to look at it. We tried a few things and eventually we figured out it was the database queries, so we rewrote them and added some indexes and it got much faster. Everyone was really pleased. I think it was one of our best improvements that year."

Problems: no "I" anywhere; "slow", "much faster" and "really pleased" are adjectives, not data; long runway; the reader cannot tell what this candidate did.

**After (about 2 minutes):**
"I sped up our monthly sales report from about 40 minutes to under 2.

I was a developer on a six-person team at a regional retailer. Every month, the finance team ran a sales report that took about 40 minutes and sometimes timed out, so they had to start again. Nobody owned it; I asked my lead if I could spend a sprint on it, and she agreed.

I first timed each step of the report and found that one query, which joined sales to products, took about 35 of the 40 minutes. Looking at how the database ran it, I saw it was reading the whole sales table, about 50 million rows, for every product category. I added an index, a lookup structure that lets the database find matching rows without reading the whole table, on the sale date and product columns, and rewrote the query to filter by month before joining. I tested it against the previous three months' reports to make sure the totals matched to the cent, and showed the finance lead the results before switching over.

The report now runs in under 2 minutes and has not timed out since. The finance lead told my manager it saved her team most of a morning each month. What I took from it is to time each step before guessing; I had assumed the problem was the report's formatting code."

Every action begins with "I", there is a baseline and an outcome, a qualitative result is backed by specific feedback, and the length is right.

## Summary

- Say "I" for your own actions. Amazon lists constant "we" as an interview mistake; OPM tells interviewers to probe it; MIT advises "I" statements. Use "the team's goal was X, my part was Y, I did Z".
- Credit others specifically, and never claim more than you did.
- Use data: numbers with a baseline, honest estimates with "about", and specific feedback when the result is qualitative. Amazon says most examples should be data-forward. Never invent numbers, and use relative figures for confidential ones.
- Length: Strayer suggests one to two minutes; Amazon says four minutes or less. Aim for about two, offer depth rather than including it, and watch the interviewer's signals.
- Practise aloud with a timer until your split looks like the plan.

## Glossary

- **"I" statement**: a sentence that names you as the one who acted.
- **Data-forward**: led by numbers and concrete facts, a phrase from Amazon's interview advice.
- **Baseline**: the value of a measure before you acted, used to show change.
- **Qualitative result**: an outcome about quality rather than quantity, supported by specific feedback.
- **Relative number**: a percentage or fraction used instead of a confidential absolute figure.
- **Index (database)**: a lookup structure that lets a database find rows without scanning the whole table.
- **Sprint**: a fixed work period, often two weeks, used by many software teams.
