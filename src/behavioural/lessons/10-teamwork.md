# Teamwork and collaboration

## Before this lesson

Read [STAR in depth](#/lesson/behavioural/star-in-depth), [Saying "I", using data, and getting the length right](#/lesson/behavioural/i-data-length) and [Building a story bank](#/lesson/behavioural/story-bank). This is the first of ten "question family" lessons. Each one follows the same pattern: what the interviewer is really testing, common phrasings, an example rubric, a weak and a strong answer written as dialogue, why the strong one scores higher, likely follow-up probes, and a short exercise.

## In plain words

A band can have four brilliant musicians and still sound terrible if they do not listen to each other. The drummer rushes, the guitarist plays over the singer, nobody agrees on when the song ends. Another band with average players who listen, adjust and support each other can sound wonderful. Hiring managers know this about software teams: almost nothing ships without several people coordinating, and one person who makes the people around them better is worth more than one who is brilliant alone.

**Teamwork questions** ask for evidence that you are the second kind of band member. They are among the most common behavioural questions anywhere. Apple's interview tips page gives "a time you worked with a team to reach a goal" as a sample question; Meta's preparation post lists collaboration among the topics of its behavioural round and says interviewers look for teamwork, leadership and mentorship qualities, shown with humility.

The trap in these questions is obvious once you see it: a teamwork story naturally uses "we", and "we" hides you. This lesson shows how to tell a story about a team in which your own contribution is unmistakable.

## What the interviewer is really testing

Under the friendly wording, a teamwork question usually probes several of these:

- **Your role in a group**: what you contributed, and whether you understand how it fitted with others' work.
- **Coordination**: how you kept work aligned (agreements, communication, dependencies).
- **Helping others**: whether you unblock, teach or support teammates.
- **Handling friction**: what you do when someone is slow, absent or doing things differently (lesson 11 goes deeper into open conflict).
- **Humility and credit**: whether you credit others fairly without hiding your own part.
- **Outcome orientation**: whether the team actually achieved something, and how you know.

## Common phrasings

- "Tell me about a time you worked with a team to reach a goal."
- "Describe your most successful team project. What was your role?"
- "Tell me about a time you helped a teammate who was struggling."
- "Give me an example of working with people from other disciplines, such as design or product."
- "Tell me about a time your team was missing a key person. What did you do?"
- "How do you make sure everyone on a team is aligned?"
- "Describe a time you had to rely on someone else's work to finish yours."

The sixth phrasing sounds general; answer it with a one-line habit and then a real example, as lesson 1 explained.

## An example rubric

This rubric is an **example** written for this lesson, in the style lesson 3 described. It is not any company's real material.

| Level | What it looks like for teamwork |
|---|---|
| Outstanding | Clear personal role; took actions that made others more effective (unblocked, aligned, taught); handled at least one point of friction constructively; measurable team outcome; credits others specifically |
| Solid | Clear personal role and contribution; worked well with others; team outcome achieved; some evidence of helping others |
| Borderline | Story about a successful team, but the candidate's own role is hard to see, or their contribution was only their assigned piece |
| Poor | No specific example; blames teammates; or the story shows working alone and ignoring others |

## A weak answer (dialogue)

> **Example (fictional).** Question: "Tell me about a time you worked with a team to reach a goal."
>
> **Candidate:** Sure. At my last job we had to launch a new feature for the mobile app, a loyalty points screen. It was a really big project and the whole team worked really hard. We had designers, frontend, backend, everyone. There were some challenges along the way, like the API was late, but we all pulled together and we managed to get it out for the launch date. The team had great chemistry and everyone supported each other.
>
> **Interviewer:** What was your role specifically?
>
> **Candidate:** I was on the backend side, so I worked on the APIs and helped out wherever I could.
>
> **Interviewer:** You mentioned the API was late. What happened there?
>
> **Candidate:** Yes, it took longer than planned, but we sorted it out with some extra hours and the frontend team was flexible.

## A strong answer (dialogue, full STAR)

> **Example (fictional).** Same question.
>
> **Candidate:** I'll tell you about launching a loyalty points screen where the frontend was blocked on my API, and how I changed the way we worked so we still made the date.
>
> *(Situation)* I was a backend engineer on a team of five at a food delivery company: two backend, one mobile developer, one designer and a product manager. We had six weeks to launch a points screen before a marketing campaign that was already booked.
>
> *(Task)* My part was the points API, the service that the app calls to show a user's balance and history. In week two it was clear that the mobile developer, Mei, would be waiting for my API for at least three weeks, which would leave her one week for the whole screen.
>
> *(Action)* First I suggested to Mei and the designer that we agree the API's request and response format before I built anything. We spent one hour on a call and I wrote the agreed format in a shared document. Then I built a mock server, a small fake service that returns realistic sample data in that exact format, and had it running by the end of that week, so Mei could build the screen against it straight away. I set up a 10-minute check-in with Mei twice a week, so any change to the format would be agreed rather than discovered. When the designer added a "points expiring soon" banner in week three, it needed a new field; because we had the check-in, we agreed it that day and I added it to the mock the same afternoon. In week four the other backend engineer was out sick for a week, so I took over his piece, the job that credits points after each order, and asked our product manager to move one low-priority report to after launch to make room.
>
> *(Result)* We launched on the planned date. When we connected the real API to the app, only two small format bugs appeared, and both were fixed within a day. Mei told our manager it was the first project where she hadn't spent the last week waiting. The points screen was used by about a third of active users in the first month. 
>
> *(Reflection)* What I learned is that agreeing the interface early is the cheapest way to let people work in parallel, and I now propose it on every project with a dependency between teams.
>
> **Interviewer:** How did the other backend engineer feel about you taking over his piece?
>
> **Candidate:** I messaged him before touching it, so it wouldn't be a surprise when he came back, and I wrote notes on what I'd changed. When he returned he reviewed my code, found one edge case I'd missed for refunded orders, and fixed it. He seemed relieved rather than annoyed, partly because the notes made it easy to pick up.

## Why the strong answer scores higher

Compare the two against the example rubric.

| Rubric point | Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|---|
| Clear personal role | "Backend, helped out wherever I could" | "My part was the points API" |
| Made others more effective | None visible | Agreed format, mock server, check-ins: Mei could work in parallel |
| Handled friction | "Sorted it out with extra hours" | Late design change handled the same day; covered a sick colleague with consent and notes |
| Measurable outcome | "Got it out for the launch" | On time, two small integration bugs, about a third of active users in month one |
| Credits others specifically | "Great chemistry" | Names Mei, the designer, the colleague who found the refund edge case |
| Reflection | None | Specific habit: agree interfaces early |

The weak answer is not a lie. The candidate may well have done everything the strong candidate did. But the interviewer can only rate what is said, and the weak answer would most likely be rated borderline: a successful team in which the candidate's own role is invisible. The strong answer is outstanding on the example rubric, because nearly every sentence is a separate, specific piece of evidence.

Notice also how the strong answer uses "we" correctly. "We launched on the planned date" is a genuine team result. "We spent one hour on a call" is genuinely joint. Every action that was the candidate's own begins with "I".

## A junior version: school project

Students often have teamwork stories that are about uneven effort. Here is a **fictional** short STAR version for a student, answering "Tell me about a successful team project".

- **Situation.** A third-year module project: a team of five building a web app that lets students swap second-hand textbooks, over ten weeks.
- **Task.** I was the backend lead, but in week three it was clear that nobody was tracking who was doing what. Two people had built the same login page.
- **Action.** I proposed a simple task board with one card per feature and one owner per card, and set it up that evening. I suggested a 15-minute stand-up (a short daily meeting where each person says what they did, what they will do and what is blocking them) on Mondays and Thursdays after lectures. One teammate, Ravi, was quiet in meetings and fell behind on the search feature; I asked him privately whether he was stuck, found he had never used the database library, and paired with him for two hours to get him started.
- **Result.** No more duplicated work after week three, we delivered all eight planned features, and the project scored 82 out of 100. Ravi finished search on his own and presented it at the demo.
- **Reflection.** I learned to make ownership visible early. I now set up a task board on day one of every group project.

This is a small stage, but every rubric point is there: a clear role, actions that helped others, friction handled kindly, a measured outcome and a specific lesson.

## Likely follow-up probes

Prepare short, honest answers to these for each teamwork story in your bank.

| Probe | What it is checking | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| "What was your role specifically?" | Your personal contribution | One sentence: your part, then your two most important actions |
| "How did you decide who did what?" | Coordination | Describe the mechanism (task board, agreement, owner per piece) |
| "Was there any disagreement on the team?" | Friction handling | A real example, briefly, with how it was resolved (or link to a conflict story) |
| "How did your teammates see your contribution?" | Self-awareness, evidence | Specific feedback someone gave, or what they did afterwards |
| "What would you do differently?" | Reflection | A genuine change, not "nothing" |
| "What did the team achieve that you could not have alone?" | Valuing others | Name what others contributed that you could not |

## Pitfalls

1. **"We" all the way through.** The single most common failure. Use "the team's goal was X, my part was Y, I did Z".
2. **Taking credit for everything.** "I led the whole thing" when you did not. The probe "what did others do?" will expose it.
3. **A story with no friction.** Real teams have friction. A story where everything was perfect sounds rehearsed or trivial.
4. **Criticising teammates.** "Ravi was lazy" makes you look worse than Ravi. Describe facts and what you did.
5. **The hero story.** You worked all night alone and saved the project. That can be ownership, but it is weak evidence of teamwork; the question asked how you worked *with* people.
6. **No outcome.** Teamwork stories still need a result.

## Your turn

1. Pick a team story from your bank. Write the "team's goal / my part / I did" sentence for it.
2. List every action in the story and mark each as "I", "someone else (named)" or "we (genuinely joint)". If more than a third are "we", rewrite.
3. Find the friction point in the story. If there is none, ask yourself whether you are leaving it out.
4. Tell the story aloud in about two minutes, then answer the six probes in the table above, each in under 30 seconds.

## Summary

- Teamwork questions test your role in a group, how you coordinate, how you help others, how you handle friction, and whether you credit fairly.
- Apple lists teamwork among its sample questions; Meta looks for teamwork, leadership and mentorship with humility.
- The core skill is making your contribution visible inside a team story: "we" for genuinely joint results, "I" for your own actions, names for others' contributions.
- Strong answers show actions that made others more effective, a real friction point handled well, a measured outcome and a specific reflection.
- Students can use group projects, clubs and part-time jobs; the rubric is the same.

## Glossary

- **Teamwork question**: a behavioural question about working with others towards a shared goal.
- **Interface or contract (between teams)**: the agreed format in which two pieces of software exchange data.
- **Mock server**: a small fake service that returns sample data in an agreed format, so others can build against it before the real service exists.
- **Check-in**: a short, regular meeting between people whose work depends on each other.
- **Stand-up**: a short meeting where each team member says what they did, what they will do next and what is blocking them.
- **Task board**: a shared list or board showing each piece of work and its owner.
- **Dependency**: a piece of work that cannot finish until another one does.
