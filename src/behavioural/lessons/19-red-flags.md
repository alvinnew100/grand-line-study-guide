# Red flags: what makes interviewers worry

## Before this lesson

Read [Handling follow-up probes](#/lesson/behavioural/follow-up-probes) and the question-family lessons from [Teamwork](#/lesson/behavioural/teamwork) to [Hypothetical questions](#/lesson/behavioural/hypothetical-questions). Most red flags in this lesson have already appeared as pitfalls; here they are collected, explained from the interviewer's side, and practised.

## In plain words

When you look at a used car, a few small signs make you walk away even if the paint is shiny: a warning light on the dashboard, a seller who will not let you see the service history, a smell of burning oil. None of them proves the car is bad, but each one raises a doubt that the rest of the visit cannot easily remove.

A **red flag** in an interview is the same kind of sign: something a candidate says or does that makes the interviewer worry about how they would behave on the job. Red flags matter more than their size suggests, because structured interviews ask interviewers to write down evidence, and a red flag is very easy to write down. "Spoke negatively about previous manager twice" goes straight into the notes and can outweigh several solid answers.

The good news is that almost every red flag is avoidable with awareness and practice. This lesson lists the common ones, explains why interviewers worry about each, shows how to fix it, and ends with a weak answer full of red flags rewritten into a strong one.

## What the interviewer is really testing, and where flags appear

Red flags are not a separate question type. They can appear in any answer, but some questions draw them out more than others, because they invite you to talk about other people, your weaknesses or your current employer. Be especially careful with these phrasings:

- "Why are you leaving your current job?" (invites negativity about an employer)
- "Tell me about a difficult colleague." (invites blame)
- "Tell me about your biggest project." (invites "we", exaggeration and confidential detail)
- "What is your greatest weakness?" (invites disguised strengths)
- "Tell me about a failure." (invites blame and minimising)
- "Do you have any questions for me?" (invites "no")
- "What do you know about us?" (exposes missing research)

In every case the interviewer is testing the same underlying things: honesty, self-awareness, respect for others, discretion and genuine interest.

## The red flags

### 1. "We" for everything

**What it sounds like:** "We designed it, we built it, we launched it."
**Why interviewers worry:** they cannot tell what you did; at worst, it suggests you did little. Amazon lists constant "we" among its common interview mistakes, and OPM's interviewer guide says to probe it.
**Fix:** "The team's goal was X, my part was Y, I did Z" ([lesson 6](#/lesson/behavioural/i-data-length)).

### 2. Vagueness

**What it sounds like:** "I did a lot of work on performance and it improved things significantly."
**Why interviewers worry:** vague answers cannot be rated above borderline, and repeated vagueness suggests either shallow involvement or exaggeration. Amazon's mistakes article lists not including enough data.
**Fix:** specifics and numbers, with a baseline.

### 3. Blame and negativity about others

**What it sounds like:** "My manager was useless", "the other team never delivered", "the client didn't know what they wanted".
**Why interviewers worry:** they imagine you describing them the same way in a year, and it suggests you do not see your own part. One of Amazon's Bar Raisers advises against negative comments about past managers or colleagues.
**Fix:** describe facts and your own actions; describe others fairly, including their reasons.

### 4. Sharing confidential information

**What it sounds like:** "I can't really say this, but our unreleased product is..." or detailed internal revenue figures.
**Why interviewers worry:** if you share your current employer's secrets with them, you will share theirs with the next company. Apple's interview tips say directly not to share confidential information or intellectual property belonging to other people or employers, and that interviewers are not interested in it. **Intellectual property** means creations a company owns, such as code, designs and inventions.
**Fix:** describe scale and shape with relative numbers ("about a 30% reduction"), and leave out names of unreleased products, customers and internal figures.

### 5. Using AI or outside help during the interview

**What it sounds like (or looks like):** reading answers generated by an AI tool, pauses while typing to a hidden window, help from someone off-camera.
**Why interviewers worry:** it is dishonest, and it means the interview measured someone or something else. Google's "How we hire" page says AI tools are not to be used during interviews. Microsoft's "How we hire" page encourages responsible use of AI tools for **preparation**, but says candidates should show their own skills without outside assistance during assessments and interviews unless explicitly permitted.
**Fix:** use AI tools, if you like, to prepare (for example, to generate practice questions), then answer the real interview yourself. If a company explicitly allows a tool in an exercise, follow its rules exactly.

### 6. Invented or exaggerated stories

**What it sounds like:** a story that is smooth on the surface and collapses under probes, or claims of leading things you only took part in.
**Why interviewers worry:** honesty is a baseline requirement; one discovered exaggeration casts doubt on everything else.
**Fix:** tell true stories. A small true story beats a large invented one every time.

### 7. Answering a different question

**What it sounds like:** asked about conflict, the candidate tells their favourite technical success story.
**Why interviewers worry:** it suggests poor listening, and it leaves the rubric for that question empty.
**Fix:** name the competency to yourself before answering ([lesson 2](#/lesson/behavioural/structured-interviews)).

### 8. Disguised strengths and no real failures

**What it sounds like:** "My weakness is that I care too much." "I can't think of a real failure."
**Why interviewers worry:** it reads as evasion or a lack of self-awareness.
**Fix:** a real, owned failure with learning applied ([lesson 12](#/lesson/behavioural/failure-feedback)).

### 9. Rambling

**What it sounds like:** five-minute answers that start long before the story begins.
**Why interviewers worry:** it suggests unclear thinking and makes collaboration slow. Amazon says responses should be four minutes or less.
**Fix:** headline first, about two minutes, offer depth rather than including it.

### 10. Repeating the same story

**What it sounds like:** every answer, to every interviewer, is about the same project.
**Why interviewers worry:** your experience looks thin. Amazon lists repeating the same example for every question as a mistake.
**Fix:** a story bank with coverage ([lesson 8](#/lesson/behavioural/story-bank)).

### 11. Arrogance, or no credit to others

**What it sounds like:** "I basically carried the team." "Nobody else understood the system."
**Why interviewers worry:** teams need people who make others better; Meta's preparation post asks for leadership and mentorship shown with humility.
**Fix:** credit others specifically; let your actions show your ability.

### 12. Unprofessional manner

**What it sounds like:** swearing, oversharing personal details, crude jokes. An Amazon Bar Raiser advises letting your personality show while avoiding swearing and oversharing.
**Why interviewers worry:** it predicts how you will behave in meetings with colleagues and customers.
**Fix:** be warm and natural, at the level you would use with a new colleague you respect.

### 13. Not preparing for the company or asking no questions

**What it sounds like:** "What does your company do, exactly?" or "No, I don't have any questions."
**Why interviewers worry:** it suggests low interest. Amazon lists skipping the chance to ask questions as a mistake and describes an interview as a two-way door.
**Fix:** research the company ([lesson 9](#/lesson/behavioural/tell-me-about-yourself)) and prepare questions ([lesson 25](#/lesson/behavioural/remote-and-questions)).

## A weak answer full of red flags (dialogue)

> **Example (fictional).** Question: "Tell me about a challenging project."
>
> **Candidate:** Oh, definitely the payments migration at my current company. Honestly, the project was a mess because our director had no idea what he was doing, and the platform team was useless, they never delivered on time. We basically had to do everything ourselves. I can tell you, between us, that the migration was for a big bank partnership that isn't public yet, it's worth about 40 million a year to us. Anyway, we redesigned the whole system and it was much better in the end. I think I was the only one who really understood the architecture, to be honest.
>
> **Interviewer:** What was your specific role?
>
> **Candidate:** I was involved in pretty much all of it.

Count the flags: blame (director, platform team), negativity, "we" throughout, confidential information (an unannounced partnership and its value), vagueness ("much better"), arrogance ("the only one who understood") and a non-answer to the role probe. Even if the work was excellent, the notes from this answer would be damaging.

## The same story, without the red flags (dialogue)

> **Example (fictional).** Same question.
>
> **Candidate:** I'll tell you about migrating our payment processing to a new provider while the old one kept running, and how I handled a dependency on another team that kept slipping.
>
> *(Situation)* I'm a backend engineer at a lending company. Last year we moved payment processing to a new provider to support a new partnership. I'll keep the partner's details out, since they're confidential.
>
> *(Task)* I was responsible for the reconciliation service, the code that checks every payment our system recorded against what the provider actually processed. It depended on a data feed from the platform team.
>
> *(Action)* Two weeks in, the feed was late, and it slipped again the following week. Rather than wait, I went to the platform team's lead to understand why. They had three other migrations landing at the same time, and our feed was fourth in their queue. I asked if it would help if I wrote the feed's data format and a test file myself, so their engineer only had to connect it. They agreed. I built the reconciliation logic against that test file and added a check that flagged any payment that didn't match within an hour. When the real feed arrived, it matched the test format except for one date field, which took half a day to fix.
>
> *(Result)* We switched over on schedule, with both providers running in parallel for two weeks. Reconciliation caught 14 mismatches in that period, all from a time-zone difference that we fixed before turning off the old provider. No customer was charged twice.
>
> *(Reflection)* What I learned is that when another team is slipping, asking what would make it easier for them works better than escalating. Their lead later asked me to write the format specification for their next two feeds.
>
> **Interviewer:** What was your specific role versus the platform team's?
>
> **Candidate:** I owned reconciliation end to end and wrote the feed's format and test file. The platform team built and ran the feed itself; their engineer, Daniel, connected it to our format.

The facts are almost the same; the red flags are gone. Blame became understanding ("three other migrations"), "we" became "I" for the candidate's own work, confidential detail was explicitly withheld, vagueness became numbers, and arrogance became specific credit.

## If you notice a red flag while you are speaking

Everyone slips. Recovering well is itself a good sign.

- **You said "we" for a while:** pause and add, "To be specific about my part: I..."
- **You were negative about someone:** add a fair line: "To be fair to him, he was balancing three launches, and I didn't understand that at the time."
- **You started sharing something confidential:** stop: "Actually, I should keep the client's name out of it. It was a large retail client."
- **You are rambling:** "Let me jump to what I did," then continue from the Action.
- **You answered the wrong question:** "I realise that was more about deadlines than disagreement. A better example of a disagreement is..."

## Likely follow-up probes when interviewers suspect a red flag

| Suspicion | Probe you may hear |
|---|---|
| "We" hides your part | "What did you personally do?" |
| Exaggeration | "Walk me through exactly how you built that." |
| Blame | "How would the other team describe what happened?" |
| No self-awareness | "What would you do differently?" |
| Thin experience | "Can you give me a different example?" |

## Summary

- Red flags are signs that make interviewers worry about on-the-job behaviour; they are easy to write down and can outweigh solid answers.
- Common ones: "we" for everything, vagueness, blame and negativity, confidential information, AI or outside help during the interview, invented stories, answering a different question, disguised strengths, rambling, repeated stories, arrogance, unprofessional manner, no research and no questions.
- Sources: Amazon's mistakes article and Bar Raiser tips; Apple on confidential information; Google and Microsoft on AI tools in interviews (Microsoft encourages responsible use for preparation only, unless explicitly permitted).
- Almost all are fixed by the habits in this track; recovering mid-answer is possible and looks mature.

## Glossary

- **Red flag**: something said or done in an interview that raises a doubt about on-the-job behaviour.
- **Confidential information**: information an employer or client has not made public and expects you to protect.
- **Intellectual property (IP)**: creations owned by a company, such as code, designs and inventions.
- **Reconciliation**: checking that two records of the same transactions agree.
- **Recovery line**: a short phrase that corrects a slip during an answer.
- **Two-way door**: Amazon's phrase for an interview as a chance for both sides to assess each other.
