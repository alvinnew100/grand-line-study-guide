# Google and Meta

## Before this lesson

Read [Why companies use structured interviews](#/lesson/behavioural/structured-interviews) and [How you are scored](#/lesson/behavioural/how-you-are-scored): much of what Google publishes about interviewing is the source of those lessons. [Influence without authority](#/lesson/behavioural/influence) covers Google's "emergent leadership" in depth, and [Hypothetical and scenario questions](#/lesson/behavioural/hypothetical-questions) covers the second kind of question Google describes.

## In plain words

Two restaurants can both serve excellent food and still run their kitchens very differently. One weighs every ingredient and follows recipes to the gram; another values speed and improvisation and expects cooks to move fast. A cook who knows which kitchen they are walking into adjusts how they talk about their experience.

Google and Meta both hire large numbers of software engineers, both use structured interviews with behavioural content, and both publish guidance. But they emphasise different things. Google has written publicly and in detail about structured interviewing, rubrics and the attributes it assesses. Meta's software engineering preparation material describes a dedicated behavioural interview and six company values with a strong flavour of speed, directness and long-term impact. This lesson covers what each company says officially, separates that from what is only widely reported, and shows how to adapt the same story bank to each.

## Google

### What Google says about its process

Google's "How we hire" page (at the time of writing) says:

- The process typically takes around **six to eight weeks**, though it varies.
- **The same rubrics** are used to assess every candidate for a role.
- Google **does not use brainteasers**, because they did not predict job performance.
- Candidates should **not use AI tools during interviews**.

### The attributes Google assesses

Google's re:Work guide to structured interviewing describes the attributes Google looks for:

| Attribute | In plain words |
|---|---|
| Role-related knowledge | Do you have the skills and experience the specific job needs? |
| Problem solving | How do you break down and reason through complex, unfamiliar problems? |
| Leadership | For individual contributors, **emergent leadership**: stepping up with initiative, working effectively across teams, developing yourself. For managers, enabling a team, shaping direction and developing people. |

The same guide gives an example rubric scale with four levels, **poor, borderline, solid, outstanding**, and describes structured questions as coming with planned follow-up questions. It describes two question types: **behavioural** (what you did) and **hypothetical** (what you would do in a realistic job scenario). Expect both.

### "Googleyness" and "general cognitive ability": handle with care

You will read on many websites that Google assesses "Googleyness" (often described as culture fit or how someone works with others) and "general cognitive ability". These terms were widely reported, including in a 2015 book by Google's former head of people operations, Laszlo Bock. However, they do not appear on the official Google pages this track's research could open. Treat them as **reported history**, not as Google's current official criteria, and prepare for what Google publishes now: role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership.

### Preparing for Google's behavioural questions

1. **Emergent leadership stories.** Google's description of IC leadership (initiative, cross-team work, self-development) maps to lessons 13 and 15. Prepare at least three: one where you took initiative unprompted, one where you worked across teams, one where you deliberately developed a skill and applied it.
2. **Hypothetical practice.** Use the six-step chain from lesson 17 (clarify, priorities, options, decide, communicate, evidence). Google's re:Work example of a hypothetical asks how you would evaluate a competitor's new pricing, so practise reasoning about business trade-offs, not only technical ones.
3. **Show your reasoning.** Problem solving is assessed through how you think. In behavioural answers, say why you chose each action and what alternatives you rejected.
4. **Expect structured probes.** Prepare the probe ladder (lesson 18).

### A Google-style behavioural question

The re:Work guide's example behavioural question asks for a time your behaviour had a positive impact on your team, with follow-ups about your goal, how teammates responded and what came next. A strong answer would come from your influence or teamwork stories, with the headline aimed at emergent leadership: "I'll tell you about introducing a shared build template that four teams adopted, without any authority over them."

## Meta

### What Meta says about its process

Meta's "Hiring process" page (at the time of writing) describes:

- A **recruiter call** about your background, goals and interest in Meta (for most roles), where Meta suggests you prepare questions about team dynamics, the role and next steps.
- **Screens** by phone or video, assessing technical background and problem solving.
- A **full loop**: a series of interviews with Meta employees.
- A typical overall timeline of **about two to three months**.
- A suggestion to research your interviewers and send a personalised thank-you message through Meta's candidate portal.

The same page describes something worth knowing about AI: for select technical interviews, Meta uses a coding environment with a **built-in AI assistant** that candidates are expected to use, and says outside AI tools are not permitted. That is very different from Google's rule. The general lesson: **follow each company's stated rules exactly, for each interview**, and ask the recruiter if anything is unclear.

### The behavioural interview

Meta's software engineering preparation post (updated in 2022) describes a **45-minute behavioural interview** alongside the coding and design interviews. According to that post:

- It aims to learn about your background, what you are passionate about in technology and the kind of impact you want to make, and whether you would do well in a fast-moving engineering organisation.
- Topics include your CV and key events in your history, past projects, accomplishments, collaboration, conflict and feedback, and technologies you are excited about.
- Meta recommends **STAR** and concrete examples; if you drift into theory, expect a request for a real example.
- Being honest about failures is encouraged; openness about setbacks will not count against you.
- Interviewers look for self-awareness, honesty and growth, and for teamwork, leadership and mentorship qualities shown with humility.

Sample questions in the post include asking about the best things you have built, what you are most proud of, what you could have done better, pushing an idea forward despite opposition, dealing with conflict, and how you like to give and receive feedback.

Because the post dates from 2022, check the current preparation material your recruiter sends; the values it mentions still match Meta's current culture page.

### Meta's six values

Meta's culture page lists six values. The titles are Meta's; the descriptions are paraphrases.

| Value (Meta's title) | In plain words | Story types that fit |
|---|---|---|
| Move fast | Act with urgency, learn quickly, clear obstacles, all heading the same way | Bias for action, small reversible steps (lesson 14) |
| Build awesome things | Make work that is not only useful but inspiring | Projects you are proud of; quality (lesson 16) |
| Be direct and respect your colleagues | Speak candidly and have hard conversations, respectfully | Conflict and feedback (lessons 11 and 12) |
| Focus on long-term impact | Take on the most significant problems even if results take years | Root-cause fixes, lasting ownership (lesson 13) |
| Live in the future | Shape the future of work, adopt new products early | Learning new technology, early adoption |
| Meta, Metamates, me | Take responsibility for the company, its mission and your fellow teammates | Ownership beyond your team; helping colleagues |

### Preparing for Meta's behavioural round

1. **Know your CV deeply.** The round explores key events in your history; be ready to explain every move and every major project.
2. **Prepare "proudest build" and "could have done better" for the same project.** Meta's sample questions invite both, and a candidate who can describe both sides of one project shows self-awareness.
3. **Conflict and feedback stories** for "Be direct and respect your colleagues".
4. **A story about moving fast** without being reckless: speed with a safety net.
5. **Passion, with evidence.** "What technologies are you excited about?" needs a concrete example of something you built or explored, not an opinion.

## Google and Meta side by side

| | Google (official pages) | Meta (official pages) |
|---|---|---|
| Where behavioural evidence comes from | Questions assessing leadership and other attributes, behavioural and hypothetical | A dedicated 45-minute behavioural interview (2022 post) plus values throughout |
| Stated attributes or values | Role-related knowledge, problem solving, leadership (emergent leadership for ICs) | Six values: Move fast, Build awesome things, Be direct and respect your colleagues, Focus on long-term impact, Live in the future, Meta Metamates me |
| Scoring | Same rubrics for all; example scale poor, borderline, solid, outstanding | Signals: self-awareness, honesty, growth, teamwork, leadership, mentorship with humility |
| Timeline | About 6 to 8 weeks | About 2 to 3 months |
| AI during interviews | Not allowed | Built-in assistant expected in select technical interviews; outside tools not allowed |
| Answer structure suggested | Structured interviews with planned probes | STAR, concrete examples |

All of these change over time and by role. Check the current pages before your interview.

## One story, two companies

A **fictional** story: you noticed that new engineers took about three weeks to make their first change to production, interviewed five recent joiners, wrote a setup script and a one-page guide, ran a trial with the next two joiners, and cut the time to about four days.

- **For Google**, aimed at emergent leadership: "I'll tell you about cutting new engineers' time to first change from three weeks to four days, which nobody had asked me to do." Expand the initiative, the interviews across teams, and what you learned about developing others.
- **For Meta**, aimed at "Move fast" and "Meta, Metamates, me": "I'll tell you about getting new teammates productive in four days instead of three weeks." Expand the speed of the trial, the urgency, and the focus on teammates.

Same facts, different headline and emphasis, as in [lesson 20](#/lesson/behavioural/amazon).

## Pitfalls specific to these companies

1. **Building your Google preparation around "Googleyness".** Prepare for what Google publishes; treat the term as reported history.
2. **Applying one company's AI rule to the other.** Read the rules for each interview.
3. **Treating Meta's behavioural round as a chat.** It is 45 minutes of structured evidence-gathering.
4. **Theory instead of examples.** Both companies' material pushes for concrete examples.
5. **No failure stories.** Both companies invite them.

## Summary

- Google (official): about 6 to 8 weeks; same rubrics for all; no brainteasers; no AI tools during interviews; attributes of role-related knowledge, problem solving and leadership, with emergent leadership for individual contributors; behavioural and hypothetical questions; example rubric of poor, borderline, solid, outstanding.
- "Googleyness" and "general cognitive ability" are widely reported (for example in Laszlo Bock's 2015 book) but not on the official pages opened for this track; treat them as history.
- Meta (official): recruiter call, screens, full loop, about 2 to 3 months; a 45-minute behavioural interview using STAR (2022 post); six values; honesty about failure encouraged; a built-in AI assistant in select technical interviews, with outside tools not allowed.
- Adapt the same story bank to each company by changing headlines and emphasis, not facts.

## Glossary

- **Role-related knowledge**: Google's attribute for the skills and experience a specific role needs.
- **Problem solving**: Google's attribute for how you reason through complex, unfamiliar problems.
- **Emergent leadership**: Google's description of leadership by individual contributors.
- **Googleyness**: a widely reported but not officially published Google term, usually described as culture fit; treat with care.
- **Full loop**: Meta's term for the series of interviews after the screens.
- **Recruiter call**: a first conversation with a recruiter about background, goals and interest.
- **Candidate portal**: a company website where candidates see their application status and materials.
