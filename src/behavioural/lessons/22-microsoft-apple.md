# Microsoft and Apple

## Before this lesson

Read [STAR variants](#/lesson/behavioural/star-variants), which introduced Microsoft's STAR(R) format with its Reflection step, and [Red flags](#/lesson/behavioural/red-flags), which covered Apple's warning about confidential information. The previous two lessons covered [Amazon](#/lesson/behavioural/amazon) and [Google and Meta](#/lesson/behavioural/google-meta).

## In plain words

When you visit a friend's family for dinner for the first time, a little advance knowledge goes a long way. One family wants you to talk about yourself and your plans; another values modesty and discretion and would be uncomfortable if you gossiped about your workplace. Neither is strange; they are different households, and the polite guest learns the house rules.

Microsoft and Apple publish fairly short, practical interview guidance compared with Amazon and Google, but each has its house rules. Microsoft recommends a specific answer format, **STAR(R)**, with a Reflection step at the end, and describes a process built around learning and collaboration. Apple's tips put unusual weight on **discretion**: not sharing confidential information from previous employers. This lesson walks through what each company says, what that means for your answers, and a worked example for each.

## Microsoft

### What Microsoft says about its process

Microsoft's "How we hire" page (at the time of writing) describes these stages: finding a role, applying, an application review (sometimes with a short screening conversation), interviews, an offer, pre-onboarding and joining.

About the interviews themselves:

- Most interview stages include **two to four conversations**, each **up to an hour**, with potential teammates and colleagues from related functions, by phone, video call or in person.
- Interviewers look at **specific past experiences** relevant to the role, **how you would approach tasks** in the role, and how your skills translate to the position. Some roles also involve writing code, sharing a portfolio or providing work samples.
- The process also considers **how you learn, collaborate and contribute to impact**, and Microsoft says its interviews use a structured, consistent framework.
- Microsoft says its process is built around **respect, integrity, accountability and growth mindset**.
- On AI tools: Microsoft encourages responsible use of AI tools for **preparation**, as long as the work reflects your own abilities, but says that during assessments and interviews you should show your own skills **without outside assistance unless explicitly permitted**.
- Accessibility accommodations are available on request throughout the process.

A **growth mindset** is the belief that abilities can be developed through effort, learning and feedback, rather than being fixed. In an interview, it shows up as stories about learning from mistakes, seeking feedback and improving.

### STAR(R): the Reflection step

Microsoft's interview tips page recommends answering with the **STAR(R)** model: Situation, Task, Action, Result and **Reflection**. The tips also ask candidates to prepare specific examples, including how they applied feedback, worked with others and adapted their approach, and say Microsoft wants to see how you think, not only what you know.

[Lesson 5](#/lesson/behavioural/star-variants) explained what a strong reflection contains: a **specific lesson**, a **changed behaviour**, and **evidence** that the change has been applied. For Microsoft, make sure every story in your bank has all three ready.

### Microsoft-style questions

Based on what Microsoft says it assesses, expect questions such as:

- "Tell me about a time you received feedback and what you did with it."
- "Describe a time you had to adapt your approach because the first one was not working."
- "Tell me about a time you learned something new to deliver a project."
- "Give an example of collaborating with someone from a different team or discipline."
- "How would you approach [a task typical of the role]?" (a hypothetical, lesson 17)

### A worked example: weak and strong reflections

> **Example (fictional).** Question: "Tell me about a time you had to adapt your approach."
>
> **Story (shared by both answers, summarised):** A developer on a team building an internal expense-claims app wrote detailed technical documents for each feature and sent them to the finance team for approval. Approvals took about two weeks each because the finance staff found the documents hard to follow. The developer switched to showing a clickable prototype in a 20-minute call, and approval time fell to about two days.
>
> **Weak reflection:** "So I learned that it's important to adapt to your audience."
>
> **Strong reflection:** "What I learned is that I was writing for engineers when my audience was accountants. I now ask, before any review, how the reviewer prefers to see things. Two of the finance staff told me they understood screens better than text, so prototypes became our default. When I later worked with the legal team on data-retention rules, I asked the same question first; they preferred a one-page table of 'what we keep and for how long', and we agreed the rules in one meeting."

The weak reflection is a slogan. The strong one names the specific mistake (writing for the wrong audience), the changed habit (asking how reviewers prefer to see things), and evidence of reuse in a different setting (the legal team). That third part is what turns a reflection into evidence of a growth mindset.

### Preparing for Microsoft

1. **Add a reflection to every story** in your bank: specific lesson, changed habit, evidence.
2. **Prepare feedback stories**, both received and given (lesson 12).
3. **Prepare adaptation stories**, where your first approach failed and you changed it (lesson 14).
4. **Prepare collaboration across disciplines**, because interviews include colleagues from related functions (lesson 10).
5. **Explain your thinking aloud** in both behavioural and technical answers.

## Apple

### What Apple's interview tips say

Apple's interview tips page (at the time of writing) covers:

- **Talk about yourself, and be yourself.** Be ready to explain your day-to-day work in your current or most recent role and how your skills match the job description.
- **Prepare specific examples** of important projects and challenges you have learned from. Apple says it already has your basic work history, so expect open-ended questions about past performance.
- **Sample question areas** on the page include working with a team to reach a goal, meeting a deadline under pressure, and areas you need to improve.
- **Role-specific exercises**: depending on the role, you may give a presentation or case study, take a technical assessment, or complete a writing or coding exercise; the recruiter will give details.
- **Accommodations** can be requested through your recruiter.
- **Arrive about 15 minutes early**, for onsite and virtual interviews alike.
- **Video interviews**: choose a quiet space, have the link ready, and check your equipment and connection.
- **Confidentiality**: do not share confidential information or intellectual property belonging to other people or previous employers; Apple says its interviewers are not interested in it.
- **Questions to ask**: about the role and what success looks like, culture and community, and how Apple's values might align with yours.
- **After the interview**: you should receive guidance on next steps; if not, contact your recruiter, and be patient while the team evaluates.

### Discretion as a theme

Apple's explicit warning about confidential information deserves attention, because engineers often do not notice when they cross the line. Things that are usually confidential:

- Names or details of **unreleased** products or features.
- **Internal numbers**: revenue, user counts, costs, growth rates that are not public.
- **Customer or partner names** under agreement.
- **Source code, designs, architecture diagrams** or internal documents.
- **Security weaknesses** in a past employer's systems.

How to tell a strong story without them: describe the **shape** and **scale** ("a consumer app with millions of users", "a payments partner"), use **relative numbers** ("about a 30% drop"), and say openly that you are leaving details out: "I'll keep the client's name out; it was a large retailer." Interviewers at a company that values discretion tend to see that sentence as a positive signal.

### Apple's three sample areas, worked

**Teamwork toward a goal.** Use a story from [lesson 10](#/lesson/behavioural/teamwork) with a clear role, actions that helped others and a measured team result.

**A deadline under pressure.** Use a delivery story from [lesson 13](#/lesson/behavioural/ownership-results). Apple's wording ("under pressure") suggests showing composure and trade-offs, not only long hours.

> **Example (fictional, short STAR).** "Two days before a store-opening event, the kiosk app I'd built for check-ins started crashing on the venue's older tablets. I reproduced it on a borrowed tablet that evening and found that the crash came from an image library that needed more memory than those tablets had. Rather than rewrite the screen, I reduced the image sizes and added a fallback to a plain list when memory was low. I tested on all six tablets the next morning with the venue manager. The app ran all day with no crashes for about 400 check-ins. I learned to test on the actual devices early, which I now do in the first week of any project for a venue."

**Areas you need to improve.** Use the approach from [lesson 12](#/lesson/behavioural/failure-feedback): a real growth area, its effect, what you do about it now and evidence of progress.

### Preparing for Apple

1. **Know your day-to-day work** and be able to describe it plainly, mapped to the job description.
2. **Prepare stories for the three sample areas**, plus your strongest projects.
3. **Audit every story for confidential details** and rehearse the "I'll keep that out" sentence.
4. **Prepare questions** on the role, success, culture and values.
5. **Plan logistics**: arrive (or log in) about 15 minutes early, with equipment tested.

## Pitfalls specific to these companies

1. **For Microsoft, a generic reflection.** "I learned communication matters" wastes the step Microsoft asked for.
2. **For Microsoft, no feedback stories.** The tips specifically mention applying feedback.
3. **For Apple, oversharing.** Unreleased product names and internal figures from past employers.
4. **For Apple, no questions about values.** Apple suggests asking how its values align with yours; prepare a genuine version of that question.
5. **For both, using outside help during the interview** where it is not explicitly allowed.

## Summary

- Microsoft: stages from application to onboarding; usually two to four interview conversations of up to an hour; assessment of past experiences, approach to role tasks, learning, collaboration and impact, in a structured framework; values of respect, integrity, accountability and growth mindset; AI tools welcome for preparation but not in interviews unless explicitly permitted.
- Microsoft recommends STAR(R). A strong reflection has a specific lesson, a changed behaviour and evidence of reuse.
- Apple: be yourself and know your day-to-day work; prepare specific examples; sample areas of teamwork, deadlines under pressure and areas to improve; role-specific exercises; arrive about 15 minutes early; never share previous employers' confidential information or IP; ask about the role, culture and values.
- Discretion can be shown positively: describe shape and scale, use relative numbers and say what you are leaving out.

## Glossary

- **STAR(R)**: Situation, Task, Action, Result, Reflection, the answer format Microsoft recommends.
- **Growth mindset**: the belief that abilities can be developed through effort, learning and feedback.
- **Cross-functional colleague**: someone from a related discipline, such as design, product or finance, rather than your own.
- **Discretion**: care not to reveal information that should stay private.
- **Intellectual property (IP)**: creations a company owns, such as code, designs and inventions.
- **Accommodation**: an adjustment to the interview process for a candidate's needs, such as extra time or accessibility support.
